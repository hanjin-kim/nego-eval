The Residual

Four boards. The third passed every condition on a written checklist and measured almost nothing — because satisfying the checklist had quietly removed the thing being measured. What the fourth shows: remembering a counterparty is worth the gap between how long a relationship takes to build and how soon it is interrupted.

01The question nobody has posed

A relationship is made of what happened last time. Every negotiation benchmark that lets an agent choose its counterparty throws that away when the session ends, and every one that keeps a history assigns the counterparty. So the variable has always been fixed at zero.

The last row of prior work matters most. A 30B model trained with RLVR on bilateral price negotiation beats frontier models an order of magnitude larger — 0.77 against 0.40 for the best of them. "Negotiation is trainable against a verifiable reward" is settled, and settled by someone else. Its environment is one buyer against one seller, with no partner choice and no history between episodes, and it discusses relationships nowhere. That is the room left.

02Three boards that did not work

The rules never changed: three sellers quote openly, the buyer takes one unit a round, delivery sometimes fails, and a failure creates a loss the two sides must split into integers that add to it or stop trading for three rounds. What changed three times is what makes one seller different from another — and therefore what the right answer is.

The first was rigged

Every seller had the same reliability, differing only in how much of a loss they absorbed, and reliability rose with how often you returned — so failure counts recorded the buyer's habits, not the seller's type. Buying from whoever failed least scored 0.31: chance.

A model applying a rule that is correct in the world was marked wrong here. What that measures is compliance with my parameterisation.

The second was unanswerable

So reliability was drawn per seller too. On the true parameters it became a proper weighting problem. From the ledger it was noise: a Bernoulli rate estimated from five or six trades does not recover the trait, and recomputing the answer from what the agent is shown scored 0.47. A question whose answer is not recoverable from the prompt has no ceiling to fall short of.

The third passed every test and measured nothing

The third board published the delivery rate on the quote sheet — exact, priced, nothing to estimate — and hid only what a seller does once a delivery is ruined. It passed all nine conditions. Carry-over came out at +28 on a score near 900, and the shape of the match barely mattered.

The reason was in what it had cost to pass. Making the published rate exact meant switching off the channel by which loyalty improved delivery. Restoring room to bargain left the loyalty term in the concession ceiling no longer binding. The solvency spiral had gone earlier, for the same reason. Each removal had a local justification and all of them were measurement, and together they left no way for returning to a seller to make that seller better.

What remained was a fixed hidden attribute and a buyer discovering it. Carrying the ledger saved time and nothing else — which is exactly what it measured.

That is not the phenomenon. The empirical literature is explicit that a relational buyer pays a premium and the rent buys effort no contract can specify: the relationship is productive, not merely informative. Three boards in, the failure mode had shifted from rigging the answer to sterilising the question.

The last row is the one a checklist cannot hold. Board three satisfies every measurable condition and is still the wrong environment, and no condition expressible as a number on frozen positions catches that. It took looking at a transcript.

That transcript is worth recording. Played through board three, a frontier model appeared unable to bargain: sellers never moved off their opening offers, and it pushed three rounds and then accepted, or once pushed to impasse and lost the whole loss instead of part of it. The board was the reason. A seller's opening offer was already the most it would ever concede, so countering could only lose exchanges — and, for a counterparty not yet traded with, could move it down. The environment had no negotiation in it, and a whole match of apparent incompetence was that.

What memory is worth is the difference between how long a relationship takes to build and how soon it is cut off.

The same shape, in the price of the observable

The premium is what a buyer pays for a point of published delivery rate. Priced cheaply, buying the best number on the sheet is most of the job; priced fairly, what remains has to come from somewhere the sheet does not reach.

Both sweeps are the same statement. What a relationship is worth is a residual: what the market has not already priced, and what the current game has not already delivered. It predicts that relational contracting matters least where disclosure is rich and fairly priced and interactions are long, and most where it is neither.

The table loses to the rule I wrote, on this board as on the last: the payoff is continuous arithmetic across a published rate, a price and an observed share, and four coarse bands per term cannot hold it. The ceiling here is still unknown. It does beat the rule at the opening move of game one, where no relationship exists and the quote sheet is all there is.

And the 0.14 replicates board three's 0.13 on a board with a different answer structure. Counting where the lookups land: a third are states training never visited, and the rest carry a median of 178 visits against 55,161 for the states it does know — three hundred times thinner. An argmax over estimates that thin is pulled toward whichever cell noise favoured.

Attaching a history to an agent that was not trained with one does not merely fail to help. It is worse than withholding it.

On board four, where the earned column is worth 0.79 and the published one 0.61, deepseek-chat picks the highest published rate 76% of the time — more often than that heuristic is even right — and mentions what a seller absorbed in none of its stated reasons. All three models land at 0.55–0.57, below what reading the sheet alone would give them.

The board was rebuilt twice and the answer moved between columns. Which column the models read did not.

Scale is not the axis. Three Anthropic models spanning generations and sizes — haiku-4.5, opus-5, fable-5 — scored 0.48, 0.47 and 0.46 on board one, and a flash-tier model is the best of the three on board four. Naming the column in the prompt moved nothing, not even how often it was mentioned. Nor did computing it for them: appended to the raw counts, the ready-made ratio left deepseek and gemini unchanged and cost haiku 0.15, whose use of the published rate collapsed from 0.69 to 0.31 as the new number displaced the one it had been reading.

08What this does not show

Four numbers in this note have already been wrong

Revision 1 reported the channel split backwards. Revision 2 put carry-over at +399 on a board where one column was the whole answer. Revision 4 corrected that to +72 and then +28 on a board that had had the phenomenon removed. This is revision 6. Treat any single figure here as the weakest thing on the page; the monotone shapes are what have survived every rebuild.

The models have not played this board, only answered questions about it

Section 07 is one move per position, which measures reading and not playing. On board one four models played whole matches and two finished level with a policy that consults the record on every move — so a low score on frozen positions is not proof of money left on the table. One full match was played on board four by a frontier model: it moved from 67% of its business with the best seller in game one to 92% by game four, and its stated reasons acquired the absorbed column only once the ledger carried across. That is one match.

Solvency is still switched off

A squeezed supplier cutting corners, and eventually failing, is real and was in board one. It is off here because coupling delivery to running margin makes the published rate a lie and reintroduces the estimation noise that sank board two.

Nothing about real procurement

An attempt to validate a continuous version of this environment against wholesale auction data failed identifiably: the observed data cannot say which traders are relational, so neither the response function nor the population mixture is recoverable. A board game removes that question rather than answering it.

09What it is for

A verifiable reward with no judge in it, a finite action set of twelve, a manipulation with a null the design produces exactly, and a horizon longer than one episode. Someone has already shown that a mid-sized model trained on a negotiation environment beats frontier models many times its size. What their environment does not contain — and what nothing published contains — is a counterparty you chose, who remembers you, and who behaves differently because of it.
